      Theorem

      Whether a graph has a vertex cover of at most k vertices — a set touching every edge — can be decided in time 2^k times linear: there are one word RAM program and one constant c such that, at every word length, given any graph in compressed sparse row form followed by the single entry k as a word x for which c(|x|+k+1) is at most 2w2 ^ w2w, the program halts within c·2^k·(|x|+1) steps and writes 111 if the graph has a vertex cover of at most k vertices and 000 if it does not.

      This is the opening result of parameterized complexity, by the bounded search tree: every edge forces one of its two endpoints into any cover, so trying both, to depth k, decides the question in 2^k branches of linear work each.

      It is also the base rung of this submission's ladder. Vertex cover in Fibonacci-of-k time and Vertex cover below the golden ratio are the same problem, the same machine, the same input format and the same shape of statement, with the base of the exponential lowered by a sharper branching rule; each sharpens the one below it without unsaying it. The textbook bound stated here is what the other two are measured against, and the base 2 is the point of this statement: no reduction rules are applied, and nothing here competes with the refined analyses that beat it.

      35# Formalization notes
      36
      37The parameter dependence is written into the bound. The usual
      38definition of fixed-parameter tractability hides it behind an
      39existential — "some computable *f* with running time *f*(*k*)·poly" —
      40and stating the theorem that way would quantify away exactly the
      41number the algorithm is famous for. The bound is the explicit term
      42`c * 2 ^ k * (x.length + 1)`: the 2^*k* is the size of the search tree,
      43the claim is falsifiable at every single *k* rather than eventually,
      44and improving the base is a change of statement rather than a change of
      45commentary — which is what the two statements above this one do.
      46
      47The program and the constant are quantified ahead of the parameter and
      48ahead of the word length. The parameter is part of the input, so a
      49single program with a single constant serves every graph *and every k* —
      50the uniform notion of fixed-parameter tractability, which is a stronger
      51statement than one program per *k*. Quantifying the program before the
      52word length says the same thing about `w`: one program that works at
      53every word length that admits its input, rather than a family of
      54programs, one of which could hide an arbitrary amount of information in
      55its literals.
      56
      57The instance word is the graph block with one entry appended. The
      58compressed sparse row block is self-delimiting — its own header
      59determines its length — so nothing needs to separate it from the
      60parameter, and the split of the word into the two parts is determined
      61by the word itself, not chosen. The parameter comes last so that the
      62graph block sits at the same offsets as in every other statement built
      63on this encoding. Both the encoding and the instance format are
      64imported from *Algorithmic Experiments on a Random Access Machine*
      65rather than restated, so that the three bounds of the ladder are claims
      66about literally the same inputs.
      67
      68The answer is stated through mathlib's vertex cover number, the
      69infimum of the sizes of all covers, so the output is a *function* of
      70the graph and the parameter and the concept surface adds no definition
      71of its own: `vertexCoverNum G ≤ k` and "some set of at most *k*
      72vertices covers every edge" agree by `vertexCoverNum_exists` and
      73`IsVertexCover.vertexCoverNum_le`. The decision problem is the honest
      74scope of the claim — a program producing a cover, or a smallest one,
      75is a different theorem with a different bound.
      76
      77One constant does both jobs. An instance is admissible at word length
      78`w` when `c * (|x| + k + 1) ≤ 2 ^ w`, written out as an explicit
      79inequality against `2 ^ w` rather than through a logarithm. What that
      80condition has to cover is every number the machine holds: the entries
      81of the graph block, which are vertex numbers, offsets and the two
      82header numbers, all smaller than the block is long; the parameter,
      83which is an entry of the word and so must be a word, since a machine at
      84word length `w` sees its input reduced modulo `2 ^ w` and a parameter
      85that is not a word is not the parameter it was handed; and the depth of
      86the search stack, which never exceeds the parameter. So `|x| + k`
      87bounds every value the run produces, and a constant multiple of it is
      88what has to fit.
      89
      90The condition is deliberately *not* coupled to the running time.
      91`c * 2 ^ k * (|x| + 1)` bounds the number of steps, not any number the
      92machine holds: the search tree has 2^*k* branches, but each of them
      93keeps a stack of at most *k* frames and vertex numbers below |x|.
      94Requiring `2 ^ k` to fit into a word would be requiring the word length
      95to be at least the parameter, and would exclude every instance with a
      96large parameter on a small machine — instances this algorithm decides
      97perfectly well, only slowly. The admissible set is therefore as large
      98as the algorithm warrants, and the claim on it is correspondingly
      99stronger; a fitting condition chosen for the convenience of the proof
      100rather than for what the machine needs would be a weaker theorem
      101wearing the same bound. The two statements above this one keep the
      102condition character for character, so the improved bounds are claimed
      103on exactly these instances and not on a smaller class.
      104
      105The fitting condition is a condition on the admissible inputs and not a
      106hypothesis of the claim, because as a hypothesis it would be empty. A
      107graph with an edge has encodings of every length, since a block may
      108list a neighbour repeatedly, so no word length accommodates all
      109encodings of a fixed graph at once and "if every instance for `G` and
      110`k` fits into a word" would never be satisfied. Restricting the inputs
      111instead says what is meant: at every word length, every instance that
      112fits is decided within the bound.
      113
      114Only encodings are admitted as inputs; the program may behave
      115arbitrarily on words that encode nothing, and on words too long for its
      116word length.
